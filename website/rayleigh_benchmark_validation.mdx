# A beginner's guide to the four-case benchmark

The differentially heated cavity is a useful first thermal-CFD validation problem: a square enclosure, two imposed side-wall temperatures, insulated horizontal boundaries, and gravity. The geometry is simple enough to inspect, while the coupled momentum and energy equations produce buoyancy-driven circulation and nonuniform heat transfer.

This article documents the `05_rayleigh_validation_sweep` study in this repository. It is both a tutorial in dimensionless setup and a quantitative report of the current calculation. It distinguishes what the data show from what they do not establish.

## 1. The benchmark question

Can one OpenFOAM setup reproduce canonical heat-transfer and velocity results over several strengths of laminar natural convection?

The reference is De Vahl Davis's numerical benchmark for air in a square cavity with differentially heated vertical walls [1]. That study reports accurate two-dimensional solutions across $10^3\le Ra\le10^6$. The present work compares four steady OpenFOAM solutions at exactly those Rayleigh numbers. It is a comparison with a published numerical benchmark, not an experimental validation.

## 2. Geometry, boundary conditions, and assumptions

The cavity has height and width $H=L=1$ m. Its left wall is held at $T_h=310$ K; its right wall at $T_c=290$ K. The top and bottom are adiabatic. All solid walls are no-slip. Gravity points downward, $\mathbf g=(0,-9.81,0)$ m s$^{-2}$.

OpenFOAM represents the two-dimensional region as a 1 m × 1 m × 0.1 m block with one cell through thickness and `empty` front/back faces. Each sweep case has a uniform $100\times100\times1$ hexahedral mesh.

| Boundary | Velocity | Temperature |
|---|---|---|
| Hot side, $x=0$ | No slip | Fixed at 310 K |
| Cold side, $x=L$ | No slip | Fixed at 290 K |
| Top and bottom | No slip | Zero gradient (adiabatic) |
| Front and back | `empty` | `empty` |

The fluid is modeled as Newtonian and incompressible. Density variation appears only in the buoyancy term (Boussinesq approximation); all other properties are constant. Radiation, conjugate heat transfer, compressibility, and three-dimensional effects are omitted.

## 3. Setting Rayleigh and Prandtl numbers

The two parameters governing the benchmark are

$$
Ra=\frac{g\beta\Delta T L^3}{\nu\alpha},
\qquad
Pr=\frac{\nu}{\alpha}.
$$

The study fixes $\Delta T=20$ K, $T_0=300$ K, $\beta=1/T_0=1/300$ K$^{-1}$, $g=9.81$ m s$^{-2}$, $L=1$ m, and $Pr=0.71$. To target each $Ra$, it changes the kinematic viscosity and thermal diffusivity together:

$$
\nu=\sqrt{\frac{g\beta\Delta T L^3Pr}{Ra}}
=\sqrt{\frac{0.46434}{Ra}},
\qquad
\alpha=\frac{\nu}{Pr}.
$$

| Case | $Ra$ | $\nu$ (m$^2$/s) | $\alpha$ (m$^2$/s) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `Ra_1e3` | $10^3$ | $2.154855\times10^{-2}$ | $3.035007\times10^{-2}$ |
| `Ra_1e4` | $10^4$ | $6.814250\times10^{-3}$ | $9.597535\times10^{-3}$ |
| `Ra_1e5` | $10^5$ | $2.154855\times10^{-3}$ | $3.035007\times10^{-3}$ |
| `Ra_1e6` | $10^6$ | $6.814250\times10^{-4}$ | $9.597535\times10^{-4}$ |

This is a dimensionless benchmark setup: varying effective transport properties is a practical way to change $Ra$ while retaining the geometry and $Pr$. It should not be mistaken for changing the temperature of a real air-filled device while holding all physical properties fixed.

## 4. Solver and discretization

All four simulations use `buoyantBoussinesqSimpleFoam`, a steady incompressible Boussinesq solver, with SIMPLE pressure-velocity coupling. The mesh and schemes are held constant across the sweep. In `system/fvSchemes`:

- `div(phi,U)` and `div(phi,T)` use `bounded Gauss upwind` (first-order upwind convection);
- gradients use `Gauss linear`;
- Laplacians use `Gauss linear corrected`.

The convective discretization is **not second-order linear upwind**. Upwind schemes can be robust but add numerical diffusion, which is relevant when interpreting wall gradients and high-$Ra$ boundary layers. The case dictionaries and run scripts are the authoritative configuration.

## 5. Quantities extracted

The dimensionless velocity components use thermal-diffusion velocity $\alpha/L$:

$$
u^*=\frac{uL}{\alpha},\qquad v^*=\frac{vL}{\alpha}.
$$

The post-processor samples 200 points on the vertical mid-plane $x/L=0.5$ and horizontal mid-plane $y/L=0.5$. It reports the positive horizontal-velocity peak on the vertical mid-plane and the positive vertical-velocity peak on the horizontal mid-plane, along with their coordinates. Reporting the positive $U_x$ branch matters: the return-flow peak has nearly equal magnitude at the opposite side of the cavity, but a different $y/L$ coordinate.

For heat transfer, temperature is sampled at $x_s=0.005$ m, the first cell-center distance from the hot wall in the uniform mesh. The local wall Nusselt estimate is

$$
Nu(y)\approx\frac{T_h-T(x_s,y)}{\Delta T\,x_s/L},
\qquad
\overline{Nu}\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}Nu(y_j).
$$

This is a near-wall finite-difference estimate and an arithmetic line-sample mean. It is not a direct integration of the OpenFOAM boundary-face heat flux. A wall-gradient post-processing method and a mesh refinement would be preferable for a formal heat-flux uncertainty assessment.

## 6. Benchmark comparison

The values below compare the sampled OpenFOAM outputs with De Vahl Davis [1]. Relative error is $100|q_{CFD}-q_{ref}|/|q_{ref}|$. Velocities are dimensionless as defined above.

| $Ra$ | Quantity | OpenFOAM | De Vahl Davis | Error | CFD peak coordinate (reference coordinate) |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| $10^3$ | $\overline{Nu}$ | 1.072 | 1.118 | 4.13% | Hot wall |
|  | $u^*_\max$ | 3.35 | 3.649 | 8.22% | $y/L=0.819$ (0.813) |
|  | $v^*_\max$ | 3.38 | 3.697 | 8.65% | $x/L=0.171$ (0.178) |
| $10^4$ | $\overline{Nu}$ | 2.326 | 2.243 | 3.71% | Hot wall |
|  | $u^*_\max$ | 16.45 | 16.178 | 1.67% | $y/L=0.819$ (0.823) |
|  | $v^*_\max$ | 19.79 | 19.617 | 0.88% | $x/L=0.121$ (0.119) |
| $10^5$ | $\overline{Nu}$ | 4.586 | 4.519 | 1.47% | Hot wall |
|  | $u^*_\max$ | 35.96 | 34.730 | 3.53% | $y/L=0.859$ (0.855) |
|  | $v^*_\max$ | 68.37 | 68.590 | 0.31% | $x/L=0.070$ (0.066) |
| $10^6$ | $\overline{Nu}$ | 8.975 | 8.800 | 1.99% | Hot wall |
|  | $u^*_\max$ | 66.88 | 64.630 | 3.48% | $y/L=0.859$ (0.850) |
|  | $v^*_\max$ | 217.86 | 219.360 | 0.68% | $x/L=0.040$ (0.0379) |

The mean Nusselt errors are 1.47–4.13%; horizontal peak-velocity errors are 1.67–8.22%; vertical peak-velocity errors are 0.31–8.65%. The errors are not uniformly small: at $Ra=10^3$, both velocity peaks differ from the reference by about 8–9%. At $Ra=10^5$ and $10^6$, the vertical peak is much closer (0.31% and 0.68%).

The complete-precision output is stored in [`05_rayleigh_validation_sweep/results/benchmark_validation_summary.csv`](../05_rayleigh_validation_sweep/results/benchmark_validation_summary.csv); the project README is [here](../README.md). The benchmark source is cited in full in [References](#references).

## 7. Flow-regime observations

<figure className="my-8">
  <img src={flowRegimesImg.src} alt="Temperature contours for the four Rayleigh-number cases, from 10^3 to 10^6" className="w-full h-auto" />
  <figcaption>Figure 1. Temperature fields change from nearly conduction-like at low $Ra$ toward wall-localized thermal gradients at high $Ra$.</figcaption>
</figure>

- **$Ra=10^3$ — conduction-dominated limit.** The mean Nusselt estimate is 1.072, close to the pure-conduction value of one. The mid-plane temperature remains close to the linear conduction profile, while buoyancy drives a weak circulation.
- **$Ra=10^4$ — stronger cavity circulation.** The mean Nusselt number rises to 2.326. Temperature contours tilt as fluid transports heat around the cavity.
- **$Ra=10^5$ — stratified core.** The center temperature profile becomes flatter than the conduction line, while stronger gradients persist near the vertical boundaries.
- **$Ra=10^6$ — wall-dominated transport.** The mean Nusselt estimate reaches 8.975. The peak vertical velocity is much larger than at low $Ra$ and occurs closer to the hot wall; the cavity core is comparatively weakly moving and thermally stratified.

These statements describe the computed fields and sampled profiles. They do not imply that the high-$Ra$ boundary layer is fully resolved, nor do the steady laminar solutions establish a physical transition threshold.

## 8. Trends across the sweep

### Heat transfer scaling

<figure className="my-8">
  <img src={nuScalingImg.src} alt="Log-log comparison of average Nusselt number against Rayleigh number for CFD and benchmark data" className="w-full h-auto" />
  <figcaption>Figure 2. Simulated and benchmark mean Nusselt numbers across the four Rayleigh numbers.</figcaption>
</figure>

A least-squares fit to the four OpenFOAM data points in log space gives

$$
\overline{Nu}=0.1331\,Ra^{0.3064}.
$$

This exponent is computed from just four discrete values. It summarizes this sweep; it is not a general scaling law and should not be presented as independent validation. A broader parameter range, consistent heat-flux evaluation, and grid refinement are needed before making a stronger scaling claim.

### Velocity growth and peak positions

<figure className="my-8">
  <img src={velScalingImg.src} alt="Dimensionless maximum horizontal and vertical velocity compared with Rayleigh number" className="w-full h-auto" />
  <figcaption>Figure 3. The two dimensionless velocity peaks strengthen at different rates over the studied interval.</figcaption>
</figure>

The vertical peak increases from 3.38 to 217.86 (about 64 times), while the positive horizontal peak rises from 3.35 to 66.88 (about 20 times). The four-point log-log fit exponents are approximately 0.597 for $v^*_\max$ and 0.424 for $u^*_\max$. As with the Nusselt fit, these are descriptive exponents over this limited data set.

The sampled location of the vertical-velocity maximum moves from $x/L=0.171$ at $Ra=10^3$ to $x/L=0.040$ at $Ra=10^6$. This supports the qualitative interpretation of increasing near-wall localization. It is not by itself a direct measurement of boundary-layer thickness: a layer thickness should be defined from a gradient or profile criterion and tested under mesh refinement.

### Mid-plane temperature

The curve at $Ra=10^3$ is close to $\theta=1-x/L$, where $\theta=(T-T_c)/\Delta T$. As $Ra$ increases, convection redistributes heat and the center portion of the curve becomes flatter. The near-wall gradients carry an increasing share of the temperature drop.

## 9. Reproduce the calculation

Install OpenFOAM.com v2512 or a compatible release with `blockMesh` and `buoyantBoussinesqSimpleFoam`. The post-processing script requires Python 3 and NumPy, pandas, Matplotlib, and PyVista.

```bash
cd 05_rayleigh_validation_sweep
./Allrun
```

The runner builds and solves each of the four cases before generating the comparison table and figures. Each case writes `log.blockMesh` and `log.foam`; sampled CSV files are under `cases/Ra_*/postProcessing/`. To process existing solutions without rerunning the solvers:

```bash
python3 scripts/postprocess_benchmark_sweep.py
```

The runner is designed to stop when a case fails; the post-processor also reports missing sample data rather than producing a success-shaped partial summary.

## 10. What this study does not establish

- **No sweep-specific grid uncertainty:** all four Rayleigh numbers use one 100 × 100 in-plane mesh. The separate grid study is a different operating condition and cannot quantify sweep error.
- **No formal solver convergence report:** the summary records final sampled fields, not a residual-based or monitored-quantity convergence history.
- **Numerical diffusion:** first-order bounded upwind convection can smooth steep boundary-layer profiles.
- **Approximate wall gradient:** the hot-wall flux uses a temperature sample at $x=0.005$ m and a finite difference. Mesh refinement and a boundary-face gradient integration are needed for stronger heat-transfer claims.
- **Discrete peak locations:** 200-point line samples limit coordinate precision; a few thousandths of $L$ is not a meaningful exact difference.
- **Steady laminar assumption:** all four cases use a steady laminar solver, including $Ra=10^6$. This sweep does not demonstrate whether the physical flow becomes unsteady or turbulent.
- **Idealized physics:** constant properties, Boussinesq density variation, two-dimensional geometry, and no radiation or conjugate conduction are assumptions.

For future studies, use a scheme/grid refinement, monitor heat flux and residuals to steady convergence, compare wall-gradient and profile-based quantities, and report uncertainties separately from benchmark differences.
