### 1. The Need for Transient Simulation

Up to this point, our simulations have utilized the `buoyantBoussinesqSimpleFoam` solver. This solver uses the **SIMPLE algorithm** (Semi-Implicit Method for Pressure Linked Equations), which is strictly designed for **steady-state** problems. It iteratively drives the solution towards a single, unchanging flow field.

However, natural convection at high Rayleigh numbers ($Ra > 10^9$) is rarely perfectly steady in the real world. Instead, the boundary layers can become unstable, shedding vortices and generating unsteady thermal plumes. In these scenarios, a steady-state solver will often struggle to converge (its residuals will oscillate) because it's mathematically fighting against the physics of the flow.

To capture this true time-varying behavior, we must transition to a **transient (time-accurate) solver**. Enter `buoyantBoussinesqPimpleFoam`.

### 2. The PIMPLE Algorithm

The PIMPLE algorithm is a hybrid of PISO (transient, strict Courant number limits) and SIMPLE (steady-state, under-relaxed). It allows us to take large time steps (even exceeding a Courant number of 1) while remaining stable, making it the industry standard for transient industrial flows.

To configure PIMPLE in OpenFOAM, we modify `system/fvSolution`:
```cpp
PIMPLE
{
    nOuterCorrectors 1;  // Number of times the entire system is solved per time step
    nCorrectors      2;  // Number of pressure-velocity coupling loops
    nNonOrthogonalCorrectors 0;
}
```

By removing under-relaxation factors (or setting them to 1.0) and letting the PIMPLE loop converge the equations at each discrete time step, we ensure the simulation is physically accurate in time.

### 3. Time Stepping and the Courant Number

In transient CFD, time is no longer just an iteration counter; it represents physical seconds. The size of the time step ($\Delta t$) determines whether the solver accurately captures the physics or crashes entirely. 

To maintain stability without guessing an arbitrary time step, we use **adjustable time-stepping** based on the **Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition**. In `system/controlDict`, we configured:

```cpp
adjustTimeStep yes;
maxCo 2;
```

This tells OpenFOAM: *"Dynamically shrink or grow the time step so that a fluid particle never crosses more than 2 cells in a single time step."*

### 4. Updating the Numerical Schemes

A transient simulation requires a temporal discretization scheme to handle the $\partial / \partial t$ terms in the Navier-Stokes equations. We update `system/fvSchemes`:

```cpp
ddtSchemes
{
    default         Euler; // First-order, bounded, implicit transient scheme
}
```

*Note: For highly accurate time-resolved studies (like Large Eddy Simulation), you would typically use a second-order scheme like `backward`.*

### 5. Analyzing the Run and Residuals

A critical part of transient CFD is proving that the solver maintains numerical stability while accurately stepping through time. Let's analyze the performance metrics from our 100-second physical simulation run on the **100x100 refined mesh** (10,000 cells):

- **Total Physical Time**: 100.00 seconds.
- **Maximum Courant Number**: 1.295. (Strictly controlled under our cap of 2!)
- **Average Time Step ($\Delta t$)**: $0.0442$ seconds. This translates to roughly 22-23 time steps per second of physical time to strictly adhere to the CFL condition!

**Residuals Analysis:**
Unlike steady-state SIMPLE solvers where we want residuals to plunge to $10^{-6}$ and stay there, transient PIMPLE residuals look like a jagged "heartbeat". This is completely normal!
1. **The "Spikes"**: Every spike in the residual plot represents the start of a *new time step*. As the fluid propagates forward in time, the initial guess for the new time step carries error.
2. **The "Drops"**: Within that single time step, the PIMPLE algorithm (using `nCorrectors = 2` and `nOuterCorrectors = 1`) drives the error down by several orders of magnitude before advancing time.
3. **Quasi-Steady Oscillations**: Notice how after $t \approx 20$s, the overall amplitude of the pressure (`p_rgh`) and velocity (`Ux`, `Uy`) residuals stop dropping and instead settle into a rhythmic pattern. This proves that the flow has reached a macro-level quasi-steady state, dominated by the continuous, rhythmic shedding of thermal plumes!

When simulating from $t=0$s (a completely still cavity), we observe a fascinating evolution:
1. **Conduction Phase (0 - 2s)**: Initially, heat diffuses purely through conduction.
2. **Plume Generation (2 - 15s)**: Within seconds, buoyancy overcomes viscosity. Sharp thermal plumes erupt from the bottom of the hot wall and the top of the cold wall.
3. **Unsteady Recirculation (20s - 100s)**: The plumes crash into the horizontal walls, driving a massive, oscillating vortex that maintains the rhythmic residual pattern plotted above.
4. **Quasi-Steady State (100s+)**: As the simulation runs past 100 seconds, the flow fully develops and the violent unsteadiness dampens. The boundary layers stabilize, and the cavity reaches a stable, time-averaged state.

**Validating against the Steady-State Baseline:**
The greatest takeaway from running this expensive transient case is comparing its final state to our previous steady-state (`buoyantBoussinesqSimpleFoam`) results. 
- Transient Max Vertical Velocity ($U_y$): **0.147 m/s** *(Steady-State was 0.143 m/s)*
- Transient Max Horizontal Velocity ($U_x$): **0.035 m/s** *(Steady-State was 0.023 m/s)*
- Transient Max Temperature Near Wall: **307.98 K** *(Steady-State was 308.43 K)*

The results are remarkably similar! This proves that while the natural convection process is highly dynamic in the beginning, the steady-state SIMPLE solver successfully captured the time-averaged physical state of the cavity without needing to explicitly step through 200 seconds of physical time.

By embracing time-accurate solvers and rigorously tracking our CFL conditions, we move away from "averaged" steady-state assumptions and unlock the ability to accurately analyze dynamic thermal phenomena!
